Subject matter expert playbook

Opinions I hold, and the evidence behind them.

A senior engineer who has no opinions is a contractor. A senior engineer with opinions they cannot defend is a liability. Here are mine, stated plainly, with the conditions under which I would abandon each one.

Part I

Technical philosophy

Eight positions that shape every architecture I put my name on.

01

Offline is a correctness problem, not a feature

Teams ship "offline support" as a cache and a spinner, then discover in the field that they built a system with no defined behaviour under partition. Offline-first means you have decided, explicitly, what happens to every mutation when the network is gone: where it is durably stored, in what order it replays, how it is deduplicated, and what happens when the server disagrees. If you cannot answer those four questions, you do not support offline — you tolerate it until a user loses data.

02

The framework is chosen by the environment, not the résumé

The most expensive mobile decisions I have been brought in to unwind were made for hiring convenience or team familiarity rather than deployment physics. Continuous sensor capture, a strict power budget, a vendor peripheral SDK, or a two-day offline window each push hard in a specific direction. Start from the constraints of the environment, and let the stack fall out of them.

03

Boundaries beat conventions

A layer boundary that only exists in the README will be violated within two sprints. Encode it: module dependencies, lint rules, analyzer errors, CI gates. Architecture that a tired engineer cannot accidentally break on a Friday is the only architecture that survives.

04

Threading is a contract, not a habit

Most "random" mobile crashes are concurrency defects wearing a costume. Declare thread confinement explicitly, make the scheduler an injected abstraction, and enforce it with analyzers. "It worked on my device" is how a race condition reaches a factory floor.

05

Measure the sequence, not the total

"Startup is slow" is not actionable. Time-to-first-frame, time-to-interactive, and time-to-usable-data are three different numbers with three different causes — JIT warm-up, eager graph construction, blocking I/O. Fix the one that dominates, not the one you noticed.

06

Instrument before you optimize, and gate on it after

Crashlytics, custom keys, non-fatals and funnel analytics are not post-launch chores — they are how you find out that OOM terminations cluster at hour four, or that a screen is abandoned at 82%. Then close the loop: a release that regresses crash-free rate does not advance its rollout, regardless of what the calendar says.

07

Write for the person maintaining it in year three

Cleverness has a maintenance tax, and in a six-year industrial codebase somebody always pays it. I optimize for the diff being obvious, the failure mode being explicit, and the architectural decision being recorded with its rationale — so the next engineer can tell the difference between a deliberate constraint and an accident.

08

Field software is judged by its worst moment, not its best

Consumer apps are judged on the happy path. Industrial field software is judged on the moment a supervisor is standing in the rain, on 1%, out of coverage, trying to close out a shift. Every architecture decision I make is stress-tested against that moment: what does the operator see, what is durably saved, what happens when the process is killed mid-write, and can they finish their job anyway. If the answer is "it depends on the network," the design is wrong.

Part II

.NET MAUI vs. React Native vs. True Native:
An Enterprise Architectural Framework

Evaluated on four axes that actually predict outcomes at enterprise scale — specifically through the lens of industrial, offline-first, field-deployed applications. Not a general-purpose framework comparison; a domain-specific one.

Every comparison of these three is worthless without a stated context, because the ranking genuinely inverts depending on what you are building. My context here is fixed and explicit: a long-lived line-of-business application deployed to field or shop-floor devices, operating offline for meaningful periods, integrating with industrial hardware, maintained by a team over a multi-year horizon. Change that context and my answers change with it.

Where this assessment comes from, honestly. My production depth is in the .NET mobile lineage — Xamarin.iOS, Xamarin.Forms and .NET MAUI — plus native Kotlin and Swift. I have not shipped a production React Native codebase. The React Native column below is an analytical assessment built from evaluating it for real projects, from solving the same class of problem in other stacks, and from reading its architecture and failure reports — not from having maintained one. I would rather tell you that than have you find out in the interview.

AXIS 1

Performance overhead

Performance overhead — .NET MAUI vs React Native vs True Native
Dimension.NET MAUIReact NativeTrue Native
Execution model Compiled IL; AOT on iOS, AOT/R8-optimized on Android. No interpreter on the hot path. Compute-heavy domain logic runs at genuinely native-adjacent speed. JS engine (Hermes) with precompiled bytecode. Business logic runs in a separate runtime from the UI it drives — fine for orchestration, poor for sustained computation. Direct platform execution. The baseline everything else is measured against.
Cross-boundary cost P/Invoke and binding-layer marshalling. Real, but explicit and predictable — you can see where it happens in the code. JSI removes the old async JSON bridge, but the boundary still exists. High-frequency crossings are where the memory and threading defects live . None. This is the entire structural advantage.
Memory & GC Managed GC with a mobile-tuned collector. Requires deliberate allocation discipline on hot paths, and awareness of the managed/native object lifetime interaction. Two memory systems (JS heap + native heap) with retention that can span both. Leaks are correspondingly harder to diagnose — a JS heap snapshot shows you half the picture. One memory model — ARC or the JVM/ART collector. Diagnosable with first-party tooling that actually understands the whole graph.
Cold start Historically the weak point; substantially controllable with AOT, curated trimming, lazy DI construction and compiled bindings. Tunable to acceptable — see the the Hilti work. Hermes bytecode precompilation and lazy bundle evaluation help materially, but runtime initialization is still on the critical path. Fastest achievable. No runtime to warm.
Sustained background load Reliable for background sync and scheduled work via platform services. Requires native modules for anything continuous. The JS runtime is the wrong place to own long-running capture. Clear winner Continuous GPS, sensor streams and tight power budgets belong here, full stop.

Verdict: for a data-heavy line-of-business app, MAUI's overhead is engineering-manageable and largely a startup-path concern. React Native's overhead is concentrated at the boundary and scales with crossing frequency — which is precisely wrong for continuous telemetry. Native wins outright on sustained load, and only there does the win justify writing everything twice.

AXIS 2

Code shareability

Code shareability — .NET MAUI vs React Native vs True Native
Dimension.NET MAUIReact NativeTrue Native
Realistic UI sharing High for forms, lists, dashboards and data entry — which is the entire surface of an MES or inspection app. Drops sharply for custom graphics. High, with more per-platform divergence than teams expect once accessibility and platform idioms are taken seriously. Zero by definition. Share the specification, not the implementation.
Domain & persistence sharing Strongest of the three One language from the ViewModel down through the domain to EF Core/SQLite — and frequently the same language as the backend team, so DTOs and validation rules are genuinely shared artifacts. TypeScript domain logic shares well and shares with the web app. Persistence and background work still need native ownership. Shared only via KMP or a hand-maintained parallel implementation. Both are real costs.
Where sharing breaks Custom rendering, deep platform integration, and any capability newer than the abstraction that wraps it. Anything continuous, hardware-adjacent, or performance-critical — all of which need a native module you own and maintain. N/A — nothing to break.
Honest expectation Very high share on line-of-business surfaces. The remainder is handlers and platform services. High share on the JS layer, with a native tail that is consistently underestimated in planning. Plan for two implementations and budget accordingly. The upside is that neither fights an abstraction.

Verdict: shareability is not a single number, and quoting one is how projects get mis-planned. Ask separately: how much UI shares, how much domain shares, and how large is the native tail. For industrial line-of-business work MAUI's domain-and-persistence sharing is the strongest of the three, and it is the layer where sharing actually pays compound interest.

AXIS 3

Ecosystem maturity

Ecosystem maturity — .NET MAUI vs React Native vs True Native
Dimension.NET MAUIReact NativeTrue Native
Library breadth Narrower for UI/consumer packages; excellent for enterprise concerns — data access, serialization, resilience, validation, identity. Widest by a distance Something exists for nearly everything. Quality and maintenance status vary enormously. Full first-party platform surface plus every vendor SDK, which ship native-first.
Industrial hardware SDKs Usually needs a binding or a thin wrapper you write and own. Usually needs a native module you write and own. First-class Scanner, printer, RFID and inspection-camera vendors ship Android and iOS SDKs. Everyone else wraps them.
Tooling & diagnostics Visual Studio, dotnet-trace, and .NET's profiling ecosystem — strong, if you know it. Flipper/Hermes debugging plus platform tools. Cross-boundary diagnosis remains genuinely hard. Xcode Instruments and Android Studio profiler — the best diagnostic tooling available on either platform, by a wide margin.
Hiring pool Smaller mobile pool, but large adjacent .NET pool that ramps quickly on a well-structured codebase. Largest and overlaps directly with web hiring. Two separate specialist hires, or one generalist per platform.
Platform-feature lag Lags platform releases; abstraction layer must catch up. Lags, mitigated by writing the native module yourself. Zero lag Day-one access to every new capability.

Verdict: React Native wins breadth and hiring; native wins hardware and diagnostics; MAUI wins the specific enterprise-data ecosystem that industrial line-of-business apps actually live in. If your app's differentiator is a vendor hardware integration, that single fact should dominate the decision — the ecosystem question is really "who ships an SDK for the thing my product depends on."

AXIS 4

Long-term maintainability

Long-term maintainability — .NET MAUI vs React Native vs True Native
Dimension.NET MAUIReact NativeTrue Native
Upgrade burden Annual .NET cadence with a predictable, documented migration path and LTS releases. Enterprise-friendly and plannable. Frequent releases with genuine breaking changes; the transitive dependency tree is the real maintenance cost, not the framework. Annual OS cycles with long deprecation windows and first-party migration guidance.
Dependency risk Low Small, mostly first-party or well-maintained dependency surface. Highest Deep transitive trees where an abandoned package four levels down can block a platform upgrade for a quarter. Lowest Mostly first-party frameworks with vendor-supported SDKs.
Type safety end to end Strong: nullable reference types, analyzers, compiled bindings. Whole classes of defect become build errors. Strong with disciplined TypeScript — but the native module boundary is only as typed as you make it, and nothing enforces that. Strong on both platforms (Kotlin null safety, Swift optionals), enforced by the compiler.
Onboarding a new engineer One language, one mental model, one debugger. A .NET backend engineer is productive fast on a clean codebase. Fast for web engineers on the JS layer; slow and often avoided at the native boundary — which is where the hard bugs are. Slowest overall, but the mental model is the platform itself, which never becomes obsolete knowledge.
Five-year outlook Microsoft-backed with a clear enterprise story. Ecosystem evolution is a real consideration; the mitigation is disciplined layering, which you should be doing regardless. Large community, active architecture evolution. Historically the migrations have been real work. Safest possible bet. The platforms are not going anywhere.

Verdict: over a five-year horizon, dependency surface area is the strongest predictor of maintenance cost I have observed — stronger than language, stronger than framework choice. MAUI and native both keep it small. React Native's breadth advantage is the same property as its maintenance liability, viewed from a different year.

The decision framework I actually use

Four questions, in order. The first one that returns a hard answer decides it.

  • 1 — Does the product depend on continuous sensor capture, a vendor hardware SDK, custom high-performance rendering, or a strict power budget? → True native for that surface, without further debate. It may be native for that module and cross-platform for the rest; a hybrid boundary drawn deliberately is a legitimate architecture, not a compromise.
  • 2 — Is this a long-lived, data-heavy line-of-business application with a substantial offline domain model, and does the organization already run on .NET? → .NET MAUI. The domain-and-persistence sharing, the small dependency surface and the LTS cadence compound over years, and the team you already have can maintain it.
  • 3 — Is time-to-market and iteration speed the dominant constraint, with a web codebase and web engineers already in place? → React Native, with one non-negotiable condition: someone on the team owns the native modules properly. The React Native projects I have seen struggle in this domain struggled at the native boundary, not in JavaScript.
  • 4 — None of the above cleanly? → Prototype the single riskiest technical requirement in two candidates for a week each. A week of evidence is cheaper than a year of regret, and the prototype answers questions that no comparison table can.
What would change my mind

If Kotlin Multiplatform's shared-domain story continues to mature at its current rate, question 2 gets a serious fourth answer — native UI on both platforms over a genuinely shared domain layer is architecturally the most honest version of what everyone actually wants. I track it deliberately, and I would rather update a position than defend one.
